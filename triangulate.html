Swell Triangulation

Works out which way a swell is coming from by when it reaches each offshore buoy, then checks that against the Neah Bay forecast. Two things make it sharper than reading peak times: it lines up the whole rise of the swell at each buoy against the others (cross-correlation), which gets the time differences to within minutes rather than hours, and it adds buoys off to the side, because the three NOMAD buoys sit almost in a straight line. On their own they time a swell well along that line but can barely tell a WNW swell from a WSW one.

1. Pick the swell

Choose a stretch that covers the swell arriving at every buoy. A big, clean rise works best. Tap a buoy to leave it in or out; at least three are needed, and at least one should be off the NOMAD line (West Washington or La Perouse Bank).

3. Check against Neah Bay

The swell-only direction at the Neah Bay buoy is shown once the swell has arrived. Neah Bay sits in the lee of Cape Flattery, which bends swell coming from the northwest, so expect it to read a little more westerly than the open-ocean direction.

How it works

A swell crosses the ocean as a long, nearly straight wavefront moving at the group speed for its period (about 2.8 × period in km/h). The arrival time at each buoy, together with where the buoys are, fixes one straight front: its direction and its speed. With more than three buoys the tool finds the best-fitting front, and the “fit within” figure says how far the worst-fitting times are from it. Because the NOMAD buoys run in a near-straight line from southeast to northwest, a buoy well off that line (West Washington, 300 NM west of Aberdeen, or La Perouse Bank) is what pins down the direction across it. The speed is a built-in check: if it doesn’t match the swell’s period, the timing has probably locked onto different parts of the swell at different buoys, or the swell isn’t a single clean train. The ± range comes from shifting each buoy’s time by up to a few minutes either way, or by half an hour for the peak and height methods, which only have hourly readings to work with. The NOMAD and Canadian buoys report height and period every hour but not direction, which is why the timing has to do the work. West Washington does measure direction, so its own reading is a useful extra check.
